## Key Idea
In a BGP interception attack, an attacker announces routes for IP prefixes it does not own, thus receiving traffic meant for someone else, and then forwards it to the real destination, so the victim sees nothing. Most defenses monitor BGP announcements, but a smart attacker can limit where its announcements spread, so the monitors see them late or not at all. The authors argue that the propagation delay in round-trip time (RTT) is a signal the attacker cannot hide: if traffic between two hosts in one country is sent through another country, the longer path raises this delay right away. For the worst-case attack between every pair of countries, most detours add enough delay to detect under ideal conditions, and the share is lower but still high with real-world measurements. Their system, HiDe, runs on a programmable switch at the network edge, passively measures RTT from TCP traffic, and tracks the minimum RTT per destination prefix in short time windows to filter out noise. When that minimum jumps above the lowest RTT possible through the threat region, HiDe blocks or rate-limits the prefix, and can optionally ping it to unblock it if the RTT drops back. On a campus trace it raises few false alarms, and it detected a test hijack within half a second, much faster than detectors that wait for BGP announcements to reach monitors.

## Critique
HiDe only works when the detour is long, which the authors acknowledge: an attacker in a neighboring country, or one that rents servers close to the victim, adds too little delay to notice. The same holds inside large countries with many close neighbors, such as Russia, where an attacker near the border can stay under the threshold. The results also drop noticeably from ideal conditions to real measurements, and coverage shrinks further as the surge threshold is raised to avoid false alarms. False alarms are rare but not zero, since congestion or a normal route change can also raise the minimum RTT. HiDe can undo them by pinging a host in the prefix, but only if that host answers back. The authors also discard flows whose minimum RTT is smaller than the distance between their reported locations allows, which can only happen if the IP geolocation is wrong. They do not say how many flows this removed or explore it further, even though HiDe uses the same geolocation data to set its threshold. Deploying HiDe needs a programmable switch, which some networks do not have, plus a software control plane that operators must configure with threat regions and thresholds and that must profile each prefix before it can protect it. Lastly, yet again, many figures sit pages away from the text that discusses them.

## Connections
[McClure et al.](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/OASIcs.NINeS.2026.12) in [my Week 4 post on congestion signals](/cs440-blog/2026/10/15/no-signal-to-rule-them-all/) found that no single signal works best everywhere, but some signals do better in specific cases. RTT is another such signal for hijack detection: it works well against long-distance interception but cannot see short detours, so the authors present HiDe as an extra signal next to BGP-based detectors rather than a replacement. HiDe also avoids active probing because some addresses do not respond, which matches what [Baltra et al.](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/OASIcs.NINeS.2026.4) found in [my Week 2 post on partial reachability](/cs440-blog/2026/10/01/partial-reachability/), although it still relies on pings to undo false alarms.
